Four in a Row Strategy: Control the Center

Four in a Row is played on a board seven columns wide and six rows tall, and players take turns dropping discs into columns. It is a solved game: with perfect play the first player wins, but only by opening in the center column. For everyone else, that gives a clear plan.

Take the center column

Every winning line horizontally, diagonally or vertically is easier to reach from the middle. A disc in the center column can be part of far more potential fours than a disc at the edge. Whenever you are unsure, play in the middle and build outward to the neighboring columns.

Think in threats, not discs

A threat is an empty square that would complete four. Two kinds matter. An immediate threat can be played next turn and must be blocked at once. A hidden threat sits higher up in a column and only becomes playable when the square below is filled. Because discs fall, the player who owns the right squares on the right rows often wins the endgame. In general, the first player benefits from threats on odd rows (counting from the bottom) and the second player from threats on even rows.

Create two threats at once

The classic winning pattern is a three-in-a-row with both ends open, for example two empty playable squares at each side of three discs. Your opponent can block only one end. Watch for the same idea on diagonals, where an open end is often hidden by a gap underneath.

Defence habits

  • Before every move, scan all four directions for any line where your opponent has three.
  • Do not play a square that lets your opponent play directly above it and win.
  • Avoid filling a column if the square on top would give your opponent a threat.
  • When behind, block the center columns first.

Practice plan

Play against the computer on Easy until you can spot every immediate threat, then move to Medium and Hard. Hard looks several moves ahead, so you will need real two-threat setups to beat it.

Common mistakes

  • Playing only on the edge columns, which touch the fewest winning lines.
  • Making a three in a row that is blocked at both ends. It looks strong but is worthless.
  • Forgetting diagonals. Most lost games are lost to a diagonal that was never checked.

A worked example

Imagine you hold the bottom of the center column and the square diagonally above-left. If your opponent ignores the diagonal, a disc in the next column creates a three on the diagonal with an open playable end at one side and a hidden end above a gap on the other. They block one, and you wait for the gap to be filled. Many games are decided this way, by patient setup rather than by a single big move.

Practice plan

Play five games on Easy, concentrating only on never missing an immediate threat. Then play five on Medium and focus on building two threats at once. Finally try Hard, and when you lose, replay the position and find the first move where a block was available.
