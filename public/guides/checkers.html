Checkers Strategy for Beginners

Checkers, also called draughts, is played diagonally on the dark squares of an 8x8 board. Each side starts with twelve pieces. Pieces move forward one square, capture by jumping, and become kings when they reach the far row. Computer analysis finished in 2007 showed that perfect play from both sides ends in a draw, so the game rewards patience and accuracy.

Remember that captures are mandatory

In standard rules, if a jump is available you must take it. This is the heart of most tactics. You can sacrifice a piece to force your opponent to jump into a position where you take two or three back. Before every move, check whether your move forces a capture and what that capture opens up for you.

Keep your back row as long as you can

The row nearest you is your defence. While those pieces stay home, your opponent cannot easily make a king. Moving them early is one of the most common beginner mistakes. Keep at least the two key back squares until the middle of the game.

Control the center

Pieces in the center have more options and can support each other. Pieces on the edge can only be captured in fewer ways, but they also have fewer moves, and a rim piece can get stuck. Try to advance in pairs so every piece is protected by another piece behind it.

Trade when you are ahead

Swapping one of your pieces for one of theirs helps the side with more pieces. If you are a piece up, trade; if behind, avoid trades and look for tactics.

Using kings

A king moves and captures both forwards and backwards, so it is worth much more than an ordinary piece. Keep kings active in the open, and avoid letting two enemy pieces trap one in a corner.

Quick tips

  • Keep pieces connected in a triangle shape.
  • Look at your opponent's forced jumps before you move.
  • Do not rush forward on one side alone.
  • Stay calm when behind; many games turn on one missed capture.

Common mistakes

  • Moving back-row pieces in the first few turns.
  • Leaving a piece unprotected so that it can be jumped for free.
  • Moving a lone piece far up one edge where it cannot be supported.
  • Forgetting that your own forced capture can be used against you.

A simple opening plan

Move the pieces from the sides toward the center in pairs, one supporting the other. Keep your formation compact so that any piece your opponent captures can be recaptured at once. Count the pieces before every exchange: two for one is good, one for two is not.

Practice plan

Start on Easy to learn the mandatory-capture rule, which surprises most new players. Then play on Medium, and try to win by forcing a jump rather than by chasing pieces. On Hard, the computer punishes loose pieces, so play slowly and protect every piece.
