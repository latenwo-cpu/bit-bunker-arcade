Minesweeper Logic: Reading the Numbers

Mine Finder, known elsewhere as Minesweeper, is a logic puzzle. Some squares hide mines; the rest show a number telling you how many of the eight neighboring squares contain a mine. You win by opening every safe square without touching a mine. Pure logic solves most boards, so it rewards careful reading more than speed.

How to read the numbers

A 1 means exactly one of its neighbors is a mine. If a 1 has only one unopened neighbor, that neighbor is the mine. A 0 means no neighbors are mines, which is why a blank area opens up automatically. Always count the neighbors you already know to be mines or safe before drawing a conclusion.

Three patterns to memorize

  • 1-1 on an edge: if two adjacent 1s are on a wall and share the same unopened neighbors, the extra square belongs to the mine side.
  • 1-2-1: along a straight edge, the mines are under the two 1s, and the square under the 2 is safe.
  • 1-2-2-1: the mines are under the two middle 2s.

Use flags properly

Flag a square only when you are certain, then use the flag to count down the numbers nearby. If a number already has all its mines flagged, every other neighbor is safe and you can open them all at once.

When you have to guess

Sometimes no safe move exists. Pick the square with the lowest chance of a mine: a square far from any number is usually about as risky as the overall mine density, while a square next to a 1 that has many unopened neighbors is often safer than it looks. Avoid guessing near corners where one mistake ends the game.

Habits of good players

Scan the whole board before each move, not just the area you just opened. Finish simple deductions first, because they create new numbers and make harder deductions easier. Take your time; accuracy beats speed.

Common mistakes

  • Guessing when a safe deduction is still available elsewhere on the board.
  • Placing a flag on a hunch. A wrong flag leads to wrong conclusions.
  • Not rechecking old numbers after opening new squares, since new information can unlock them.

A worked example

Suppose a 2 has exactly two unopened neighbors. Both must be mines. Now look at a 1 next to one of those mines: that 1 is satisfied, so all its other unopened neighbors are safe. Open them, and you will probably reveal new numbers that continue the chain. This chain reaction is how nearly all boards are solved.

Practice plan

Begin with a small board and no time pressure. When you can finish it without a guess, move to larger boards and add more mines.
